# SCP-6048

**Item #:** SCP-6048

**Object Class:** Euclid

**Special Containment Procedures:** SCP-6048 is a component of the Shipping Forecast, a maritime weather bulletin issued by the UK Met Office and broadcast four times daily on BBC Radio 4. The Foundation does not attempt to prevent SCP-6048 from being broadcast. Two such attempts have been made (see Addendum 6048-C). Neither is to be repeated.

Containment procedures address the *concealment* of SCP-6048's anomalous nature, and the welfare of the single Person of Interest associated with it.

The Foundation maintains one embedded asset within BBC Radio 4 continuity staff, one within the BBC Sound Archive, and one within the Met Office's Marine Forecasting division. Their function is not to intervene but to ensure that any internal BBC or Met Office enquiry into the discrepancy described below is answered, as it has been since at least 1961, with a standard form letter explaining that there are thirty-one sea areas. The form letter is genuine. It was written by the BBC. The Foundation has not needed to alter it.

Public listener correspondence to the BBC regarding SCP-6048 is monitored. Since records begin, forty-three such letters have been received. All have been answered with the form letter. None of the correspondents has pursued the matter further. This pattern is consistent with the Foundation's broader finding (Addendum 6048-D) that SCP-6048 is not, in practice, noticed.

The discoverer of SCP-6048, **PoI-6048** (Mrs. Margaret ████████, of ████████, Aberdeenshire), is under Class-C monitoring. She has been contacted under a cover identity — a BBC Sound Archive researcher — and has been informed that her notebooks are of historical interest to the Corporation. This is true. She has agreed to bequeath them to the BBC. The bequest will be intercepted. She is aware of a good deal more than the cover identity was designed to assume, and the Foundation has, after review, decided that this does not matter. See Addendum 6048-F.

---

**Description:** SCP-6048 is the designation given to a **thirty-second sea area** read aloud during the Shipping Forecast on BBC Radio 4.

The Shipping Forecast is issued by the Met Office for thirty-one named sea areas surrounding the British Isles, from Viking in the northeast to Southeast Iceland in the northwest, and is read in a fixed clockwise order. The text is supplied to the BBC by the Met Office. The announcer reads it. The forecast is subject to a strict word limit so that the broadcast fits its scheduled slot.

In every recorded broadcast of the late (00:48) Shipping Forecast examined by the Foundation since 1955, the announcer reads a thirty-second area. It is read between Bailey and Fair Isle. It receives a full forecast — wind direction and force, sea state, weather, visibility — in the same format and the same voice as the thirty-one others. When gale warnings are in force for it, it is listed among the gale warnings. The forecast takes between nine and twelve seconds to read.

The area is not in the text issued by the Met Office. It is not in the script on the announcer's desk. The announcer does not remember reading it. The broadcast does not overrun.

The area does not appear on any Met Office chart. It does not appear in any edition of the Admiralty list of sea areas, in any BBC style guide, or in any atlas. Its name, being spoken and never written by whoever issues it, has no canonical spelling. The forty-three listener letters in BBC correspondence files spell it fourteen different ways. The Foundation uses the spelling adopted by PoI-6048, whose notebooks constitute the only continuous record of it in existence, and renders it in this document as **████**.

████ is, by every available test, a real place with real weather. The Foundation has been unable to find it.

## Addendum 6048-A — Discovery

SCP-6048 came to Foundation attention in October 2019 by way of a letter to the BBC.

The letter was from PoI-6048, then aged seventy-two, a retired school secretary and widow living in a coastal town in Aberdeenshire. It was handwritten, two pages, and courteous. It explained that she had been listening to the 00:48 Shipping Forecast every night since January 1979, and that since the same date she had transcribed it, in full, into a series of ruled notebooks. She had recently been given a gift — an illustrated book about the Shipping Forecast, of the kind sold in bookshops around Christmas — and had noticed that the map in the book showed thirty-one sea areas.

Her notebooks, she wrote, had thirty-two. They had always had thirty-two. She enclosed a photocopy of a representative page and asked whether the BBC could tell her which was correct.

The letter was received by a junior producer in the Radio 4 continuity department, who — according to her own later account — assumed it was an error on the listener's part, drafted the form letter, and then, "because it was a quiet afternoon and the handwriting was so neat," pulled the previous night's broadcast from the archive server and listened to it with the Met Office text open beside her.

She heard the announcer read Bailey. She heard the announcer read ████. She heard the announcer read Fair Isle.

She listened to it eleven times. She then went and found the announcer, who had read the forecast live the previous night and was in the building. The announcer listened to the recording once, went pale, and asked to be excused. The producer escalated the matter to her department head, who escalated it to the BBC's Editorial Policy unit, where it was received by a Foundation embedded asset and quietly redirected.

The junior producer was told that the matter had been resolved as a technical fault. She has since been promoted twice and has not raised it again. The announcer requested, and was granted, a transfer to daytime continuity. She has been interviewed (Addendum 6048-B).

PoI-6048 was sent the form letter. She wrote back thanking them for it. She did not dispute it. A Foundation agent posing as a Sound Archive researcher visited her three weeks later to examine the notebooks.

There are fourteen of them. They cover every night from 4 January 1979 to the present, with the exception of eleven nights on which she was in hospital, each marked *"in hospital — not heard."* Each entry records the date, the general synopsis, and the full forecast for thirty-two areas, in a clear schoolroom hand that has not deteriorated appreciably in forty-six years. The agent photographed every page. There are approximately 16,700 entries.

The BBC Sound Archive, by comparison, holds intermittent recordings of the late forecast. Complete runs exist only since 2003. The Foundation's understanding of SCP-6048 before that date is derived almost entirely from PoI-6048's notebooks.

## Addendum 6048-B — The Announcer

The following is excerpted from the Foundation's interview with Ms. ████, a Radio 4 continuity announcer of nineteen years' standing, conducted under cover of a BBC internal review. She had read the late forecast approximately 1,400 times.

> **Interviewer:** You've heard the recording.
>
> **Ms. ████:** I've heard it.
>
> **Interviewer:** Can you describe what you hear?
>
> **Ms. ████:** It's me. It's — it's not a splice, it's not an edit, I know what an edit sounds like. It's my voice, it's my breath, it's the way I'd pace it. I go *Bailey*, and I do the pause, and then I say a word I have never said in my life and I give it a forecast and I say *Fair Isle* and carry on. And the read is *good*. That's the thing. I'd be pleased with that read.
>
> **Interviewer:** You don't remember reading it.
>
> **Ms. ████:** I don't remember reading it and I'd stake my house I didn't. I've read that script — I read it that night, I had the paper in front of me, I can tell you the paper said Bailey and then Fair Isle because it always does. There's nothing between them. There's never been anything between them.
>
> **Interviewer:** Have you listened to any of your other broadcasts?
>
> **Ms. ████:** *(pause)* Six of them. They've all got it.
>
> **Interviewer:** How does that make you feel?
>
> **Ms. ████:** *(long pause)* You know how, when you're reading the forecast at that hour — it's a quarter to one, the building's empty, the light's on in the studio and nowhere else — and there's a thing they tell you when you start, which is that you're not reading it for the people in bed. You're reading it for the people who are actually out there. On the water. In the dark. That's who it's for. The people in bed are just — listening in.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> I've always thought that was a nice thing to say. I'd never thought about who *else* might be listening in.

Ms. ████ agreed, at Foundation request, to read the late forecast on one further occasion under observation. She read it live with a mechanical tally counter in her left hand, clicking once per area. She counted thirty-one. The Foundation's studio observer, seated beside her, counted thirty-one. The broadcast recording contains thirty-two.

Ms. ████ was not asked to read the late forecast again.

## Addendum 6048-C — Intervention Attempts

The Foundation has twice attempted to prevent the broadcast of SCP-6048. Both attempts are documented here as a matter of record and as a caution against a third.

**Attempt 1 (March 2020).** The live announcer was replaced with a pre-recorded read of the Met Office text, produced in a Foundation-controlled studio by a Foundation announcer, verified by four independent listeners as containing thirty-one areas, and patched into the Radio 4 transmission chain at 00:48. The broadcast, as received by listeners and by the Foundation's own monitoring, contained thirty-two areas. The thirty-second was read in the Foundation announcer's voice. The master recording, re-examined immediately after transmission, contained thirty-one.

**Attempt 2 (November 2021).** With the cooperation of the embedded Met Office asset, the late forecast text was withheld from the BBC entirely for one night, and the announcer was supplied instead with a short notice stating that the Shipping Forecast was temporarily unavailable owing to a technical fault. The announcer read the notice. The announcer then, according to the broadcast recording, read the following:

> *"And there are warnings of gales in ████. ████. Southwesterly gale 8 to storm 10, veering westerly, decreasing 6 later. Sea state very high. Squally showers, thundery at times. Visibility poor, occasionally very poor."*

The announcer then read the closing line — *"And that is the end of the Shipping Forecast"* — and continued into the scheduled programme. The broadcast did not overrun.

The announcer on that occasion was a Foundation asset who had been briefed on the experiment and instructed to remain alert for any impulse to speak beyond the notice. Her post-broadcast report reads, in part:

> *I was awake for that one. I was* trying *not to. I read the notice, I put the paper down, I had my hand on the fader, and I heard myself say the name. I was listening to it as it happened. It was like hearing someone else read in my voice, except I could feel my mouth doing it. I don't think I could have stopped it. I don't think it was asking.*

The Foundation's conclusion from the two attempts is that SCP-6048 is not a corruption of the Shipping Forecast. It is a forecast in its own right, issued independently, by an issuer the Foundation has not identified, and the BBC's late broadcast is simply the channel by which it is currently delivered. Withholding the Met Office forecast does not withhold SCP-6048. It merely removes the thirty-one areas in which it is ordinarily embedded, and leaves the one.

The gale in ████ that night was, by the standards of its own forty-six-year record, the third most severe on file.

## Addendum 6048-D — Characterisation

**1. Format.** The SCP-6048 forecast uses the full Shipping Forecast vocabulary — the Beaufort scale, the standard sea-state terms, the standard visibility bands, the standard timing words ("later," "soon," "imminent"). It uses no term that the Met Office does not use. It has never, in the record, deviated from format.

**2. Timing.** The thirty-second area adds between nine and twelve seconds to the read. The late forecast slot is not overrun; the following programme begins on schedule. Frame-accurate analysis of BBC transmission logs confirms that the broadcast, as transmitted, is exactly the length it would be without the additional area, and also contains the additional area. The ten seconds are, in a sense the Foundation has not been able to resolve, accommodated. The Department of Anomalous Geography has requested a copy of this file, citing a possible relationship with SCP-3340. The request has been granted. No conclusion has been reached.

**3. Meteorology.** Foundation meteorologists have analysed the complete 16,700-entry record. The weather is *coherent*. It is autocorrelated night to night in the way real weather is. It shows a clear annual cycle, with winter gales and summer calms. Frontal systems can be traced passing through it over periods of two to four days. Its climatological profile — dominant westerlies, high frequency of gale-force winds, persistent poor visibility in autumn — is that of a temperate open-ocean location in the North Atlantic at approximately 56° to 59° North.

It is, in other words, exactly the weather one would expect for a sea area situated between Bailey and Fair Isle, if there were one.

The SCP-6048 record does *not* correlate with the actual observed weather in Bailey, Hebrides, Fair Isle, Rockall, or Faeroes at better than r ≈ 0.31 over the full record. For comparison, adjacent real sea areas correlate with one another at r ≈ 0.6 to 0.7. ████ is near those areas. It is not in them. Its weather is its own.

**4. Perception.** The Foundation conducted a survey of 1,000 self-described regular listeners to the late forecast. Asked to list the sea areas from memory, 38% included a thirty-second area, spelled fourteen ways. None could locate it on a map. When shown the official map, subjects typically paused, said something equivalent to *"oh — I must have been thinking of somewhere else,"* and moved on. No subject pursued the matter. No subject appeared distressed.

This is consistent with the BBC correspondence record — forty-three letters in sixty-four years, each satisfied by a form reply — and with the Foundation's assessment that SCP-6048 has been broadcast nightly to an audience of hundreds of thousands, for at least seven decades, without being noticed by anyone who was not writing it down.

**5. Onset.** The earliest BBC recording of the late forecast that the Foundation has been able to obtain, from 1949, contains thirty-one areas. The earliest recording containing thirty-two is from November 1955. No surviving recording exists from the interval. The Foundation has been unable to identify any event, meteorological, maritime, or otherwise, that corresponds to it.

## Addendum 6048-E — Correlation

In 2022, a Foundation analyst in the Department of Anomalous Geography, working from the SCP-6048 gale-warning record and the UK Marine Accident Investigation Branch archive, identified the following.

Between 1955 and 2024, sixty-one vessels were lost with all hands in the sea areas of Hebrides, Bailey, Rockall, Malin, Fair Isle, and Faeroes, whose wrecks were **never subsequently located** despite search. Of those sixty-one, **fifty-eight** were lost on nights when a gale warning was in force for ████.

Over the same period, one hundred and twelve vessels were lost in the same areas whose wrecks *were* subsequently located. For those vessels, there is no correlation with the ████ gale record whatsoever.

The analyst's report was brief. Its conclusion is reproduced here in full:

> *I want to be careful about what I am claiming. I am not claiming that the forecast causes losses. The forecast is, by its own account, a forecast; it describes weather that is going to happen. I am not claiming that ████ is a physical location that a vessel can sail into, because we have looked for it, with ships and with satellites, and it is not there. Bailey is there. Fair Isle is there. Between them is more of the same sea.*
>
> *What I am claiming is narrower. Fifty-eight vessels went out and did not come back and were not found, and on each of those nights, a voice on the wireless was warning of gales in a place we cannot find. And on the nights when the sea gave the ships back — when there was a wreck, and a search, and a position — the voice was not warning of anything.*
>
> *The Shipping Forecast is issued for the safety of those at sea. That is what it is for. I think ████ is issued for the same reason. I think it is issued for those at sea in ████. I do not think they are dead, exactly. I think they are somewhere with weather, and someone is telling them what it will be like tomorrow, and we can hear it because we are in range.*
>
> *I have not been able to sleep with the radio on since I worked this out. I am recording that as a matter of professional disclosure and not as a complaint.*

The Foundation's official position remains that the correlation is *unexplained*. Internally, no member of the SCP-6048 research group disputes the analyst's reading of it.

## Addendum 6048-F — PoI-6048

PoI-6048's late husband, Mr. Andrew ████████, was a deckhand aboard the ████████-registered trawler *████████*, which sailed from ████████ on 14 January 1978 and was lost with all hands in sea area Bailey during a severe gale on the night of 17–18 January. She was thirty-one. They had been married four years. The wreck was never found.

PoI-6048 began transcribing the late Shipping Forecast on 4 January 1979.

In the course of the Foundation's second visit to her home, in early 2023, the visiting agent — still under Sound Archive cover — asked, as gently as the cover permitted, why she had begun. The agent had been instructed to ascertain whether PoI-6048 had identified the thirty-second area as anomalous, and if so, whether she was distressed by it. The relevant portion of the transcript follows.

> **Agent:** Can I ask what got you started? With the notebooks.
>
> **PoI-6048:** You can ask. *(pause)* I couldn't sleep, that first year. So I'd have the wireless on. And you get to know it, don't you. The tune, and then the voice. I started writing it down because it gave my hands something to do while I listened. That's all it was, to start with.
>
> **Agent:** And you noticed the — the extra one. The thirty-second.
>
> **PoI-6048:** Oh, the first month. I'm not daft, love. Thirty-one on his chart — I had his chart, from the boat, it's on the wall in the back room — and thirty-two on the wireless. I knew what it was the first week. Wrote to the BBC about it in 1981 and got a very nice letter back saying I must be mistaken. Didn't push. Didn't see the point.
>
> **Agent:** Can I ask what you knew it was?
>
> **PoI-6048:** *(long pause)* It's where he is.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> I don't know if it's a place. I don't think it's a place, not the way this is a place. I don't think you could sail there. But it's got weather. It's got weather every night, and it's always the same sort of weather he'd have been out in, and some nights it's bad and some nights it's not so bad, and somebody is on the wireless telling me which. And I would rather know than not. You'd want to know. If it was yours.
>
> **Agent:** *(pause)* Yes. I think I would.
>
> **PoI-6048:** So that's it. That's why. It's not for the Met Office and it's not for the BBC. I write it down so somebody has. In case it matters to somebody that somebody did.
>
> **Agent:** Why did you write to us again? In 2019?
>
> **PoI-6048:** Because I'm seventy-six and I've got fourteen notebooks in a cupboard and I'd like them to go somewhere when I go. Not for the weather. You've got the weather. For him.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> And because I wanted to see if they'd send the same letter. They did. It's a good letter. I've got both of them.

The agent's post-visit report recommended, in strong terms, that no further attempt be made to assess PoI-6048's understanding of SCP-6048, on the grounds that her understanding was already complete and considerably more settled than the Foundation's own. The recommendation was accepted.

PoI-6048 was, at the Foundation's direction, sent a genuine letter from the BBC Sound Archive formally accepting her bequest and thanking her for forty-four years of transcription. The letter was drafted by the embedded asset and signed by the Head of Archive, who was told only that the notebooks were a unique amateur record of the late forecast. This is true. She has the letter. The Foundation is informed it is on the wall in the back room, beside the chart.

## Addendum 6048-G — Ethics Committee Finding

The Ethics Committee reviewed SCP-6048 in 2023, following Addendum 6048-E, to determine whether the Foundation had any obligation to attempt further intervention — either to suppress the broadcast, or to disclose its nature to the families of the fifty-eight.

The finding is reproduced in part.

> *The Committee has considered whether SCP-6048 causes harm. It does not, so far as we can determine. It has been broadcast nightly for seventy years to an audience that does not notice it, and the one person who has noticed it has arranged her life around it in a manner we would be hard-pressed to improve upon.*
>
> *The Committee has considered whether the Foundation should attempt to stop it. Two attempts have been made. Both established that the forecast is not ours to stop, and the second established that it does not need the other thirty-one areas in order to be read. We are not persuaded that a third attempt would produce a different result, and we are concerned that it might produce a worse one.*
>
> *The Committee has considered whether the families of the fifty-eight should be told. We have concluded that we do not know what we would be telling them. We do not know that ████ is a place. We do not know that anyone is there. We know only that it has weather, and that the weather is announced. To tell a bereaved family that their relative may be somewhere with weather would be to hand them a question we cannot answer and then leave. Mrs. ████████ arrived at her own understanding over four decades and on her own terms. We are not entitled to impose it on anyone else in an afternoon.*
>
> *The Shipping Forecast is issued for the safety of those at sea. The Committee has come to accept that SCP-6048 is issued for the same purpose, for a sea we cannot chart, and that it is also — whether by design or otherwise — for those who wait. It has been doing this job, so far as we can tell, competently and without interruption since 1955. We see no reason to interfere with it.*
>
> *The Committee recommends no action. The Committee recommends that the broadcast continue.*

The recommendation was adopted unanimously.

## Addendum 6048-H — Note

The fourteen notebooks will, in due course, come to the Foundation. The Sound Archive cover will accept them, and they will be transferred to Site-██ and stored under conditions appropriate to a primary historical record.

The SCP-6048 research group has, however, submitted a proposal — currently under review, and expected to be approved — that the transcription not be allowed to lapse. The proposal notes that the Foundation possesses a complete recorded run of the broadcast since 2003 and could, if it wished, generate the transcript automatically. It nonetheless requests that a member of the group be assigned, on a rotating basis, to sit up each night with the wireless on, and write the forecast for ████ down by hand, in a ruled notebook, in the same format.

The proposal offers no operational justification for this. Its closing paragraph reads:

> *She said she writes it down so that somebody has. We would like there to continue to be a somebody.*
